Remodel rough-in: where plumbing gets decided

Rough-in is the stage when pipes are set in open walls and floors before they are closed up. It fixes where every drain, vent and supply line ends, so a shower valve placed two inches off or a sink drain in the wrong stud bay is expensive to correct later.

For a kitchen, that means sink, dishwasher and ice maker lines. For a bath, it means a shower valve, tub spout, toilet flange and lavatory. For a laundry, it means a washer box and a standpipe with a trap. Bring your fixture selections, or the spec sheets, before rough-in starts.

An ADU or addition adds a separate question: whether existing lines can handle the extra load, or whether new supply and drain connections are needed. We can look at that during a site visit.

Gas line work: safety comes before any plumber call

Smell gas? Leave first

If you smell gas, leave the building immediately, call 911, then call PG&E at 1-800-743-5000. Do not use matches, light switches or flashlights inside, and do not call a plumber before you have called 911 and PG&E. Read the PG&E gas leak safety page for the full steps.

We do not promise a response time for gas leaks. That job belongs to the fire department and PG&E first.

Planned gas piping: ranges, dryers, fire pits and outdoor kitchens

Installing or extending a gas line is licensed work that usually requires a permit and an inspection. Permit rules vary, so ask the Lafayette building department before work starts, and ask your plumber to pull the permit if one is needed.

The pipe is sized for the total load of the appliances it feeds, run with the proper material and protected where it passes through walls and framing. After installation, the line is pressure tested before it is connected, then leak-checked at each fitting. PG&E handles the meter and restoring service if gas was shut off at the meter.

Call 811 before any digging near buried lines.

Your installation project from first call to final inspection

Most installs follow this path.

  1. Site look: we check the existing supply, drains, vents, shutoffs and the room where the fixture will go.
  2. Fixture and scope list: you choose fixtures, we confirm they fit and list what else is required, such as new valves, venting or a wall opening.
  3. Permit check: we tell you whether a permit applies and who pulls it.
  4. Install and test: supply lines are pressure tested, drains are filled and checked, and fixtures are set and tested before closing walls.
  5. Inspection and handoff: if an inspection is required, it happens before walls are closed, and we show you the shutoffs.

Code, permits and inspection: ask before you build

California Plumbing Code

California plumbers follow the California Plumbing Code, Part 5 of Title 24, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). City of Lafayette lists water heater repair and replacement at single-family homes as exempt from a building permit, but for fixtures, relocations and gas lines, permit rules vary, so ask the Lafayette building department.

Lafayette contracts with the Contra Costa County Building Inspection Department for plan check and inspection. Verify any plumber you hire at the CSLB license check, and look for a C-36 plumbing classification.

Installation cost: what moves the number

The biggest drivers are replacing a fixture in place versus moving it, how much wall or floor must open, and the fixture itself. Premium valves, wall-hung toilets and custom sinks cost more than standard ones, and a water heater swap that needs a new vent or gas line adds work.

Access also matters: slab floors, tight crawlspaces and finished walls take more time than open framing. Permits, inspections and patching are separate lines on some projects.

Insurance rarely pays for planned upgrades. Call for a clear price before work starts, and if old supply pipe is the real issue, look at repiping first.
